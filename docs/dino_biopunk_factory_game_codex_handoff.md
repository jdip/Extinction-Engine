**Codex handoff document**  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 2026-06-28  
**Owner:** Joseph DiPaolo

## 1. One-line pitch

A first-person, hosted-multiplayer factory-defense survival game where a stranded faster-than-light test pilot industrializes the dinosaur age, building a hybrid machine-and-biological fortress to survive escalating prehistoric attacks and research a way back to the present.

A punchier shorthand:

> **Satisfactory meets Factorio meets Jurassic Park, but the factory slowly becomes a living organism.**

---

## 2. Core premise

The player is a pilot testing an experimental faster-than-light engine. The test fails catastrophically and throws the player hundreds of millions of years into Earth’s past, when dinosaurs dominate the ecosystem.

The ship survives, but the FTL/time-drive is destroyed. The onboard ship AI remains functional, but it has limited fabrication capacity. The AI can guide the player, run research, reconstruct partial future technologies, and help automate production. The player must build an industrial base from prehistoric resources to eventually create a temporal return system.

The world is hostile, not because dinosaurs are “evil,” but because the player is disrupting the prehistoric ecosystem. Mining, power generation, noise, heat, lights, chemical waste, animal farms, blood scent, and temporal anomalies all attract different kinds of threats.

The end goal is to build enough industrial, biological, defensive, and computational infrastructure to research and activate a way home.

## 3. Design pillars

### 3.1 First-person industrial survival

The game should feel like a 3D first-person factory game with real danger. Early on, the player is personally vulnerable and must fight, flee, repair, and improvise.

The first hours should feel like:

> “I am one person with a damaged spaceship, a portable fabricator, and the jungle is louder than my machines.”

### 3.2 Automation as survival

Automation is not only convenience. It is how the player survives.

The factory produces:

- ammo
- walls
- turrets
- repair materials
- fuel
- sensors
- biological feedstock
- wetware control modules
- replacement parts
- time-machine components

A guiding design rule:

> **Every combat problem eventually becomes a logistics problem.**

### 3.3 Combat-forward factory gameplay

The game is inspired by Factorio and Satisfactory, but should lean harder into combat and base defense than either. It should blur the line with tower defense.

Combat should include:

- personal first-person combat
- automated turrets
- kill corridors
- fences, walls, gates, trenches, decoys
- resource-fed defenses
- attack waves generated by ecological pressure
- species-specific enemy roles
- late-game siege events

### 3.4 Dinosaurs as ecosystem and resource

Dinosaurs are not only enemies. They are:

- threats
- neutral wildlife
- farm animals
- environmental hazards
- biological resources
- living industrial machinery
- wetware computation substrates
- sources of ecological backlash

This is central to the game’s identity.

### 3.5 Biopunk instead of rebuilding modern industry

Do not make the player recreate a full modern semiconductor supply chain. Instead, lean into biopunk.

The ship AI cannot realistically rebuild advanced chip fabrication from scratch at useful scale. Instead, it uses prehistoric biology as a workaround:

> **Metal machines provide force. Living systems provide adaptation. The ship AI provides intent.**

Dinosaur tissue, organs, glands, neural structures, and genetically modified organisms become major industrial components.

### 3.6 Hosted multiplayer as a core fantasy

The game should be designed from the start around hosted multiplayer worlds.

A strong version of the fantasy:

> Players share a persistent deep-time world, build a living dino-factory fortress together, and log back in to see what expanded, mutated, broke, escaped, or started attracting apex predators.

This has major architectural consequences: the Rust server should be authoritative from day one.

## 4. Target player experience

The ideal emotional arc:

1. The player crash-lands and barely survives small predators with improvised tools.
2. The player builds crude miners, smelters, belts, walls, and manually loaded turrets.
3. The player captures herbivores and starts ranching them for feedstock, eggs, manure, hide, bone, and biochemical resources.
4. Dino farms attract predators, forcing stronger perimeter defense.
5. The player begins gene-splicing dinosaurs into specialized chemical and material factories.
6. The player discovers that neural tissue can replace some advanced electronics.
7. Raptor brains improve turret targeting, pterosaur cortices improve drone navigation, herd brains optimize logistics.
8. The base becomes a mix of steel, concrete, bone, vats, nerve conduits, slurry pipes, floodlights, turrets, and living walls.
9. Temporal research destabilizes the local ecosystem and attracts increasingly severe attacks.
10. The final return-home system charges while the entire ecosystem attacks the factory-fortress.

The player should eventually be able to say:

> “I started with a flare gun and a broken spaceship. Now my automated railgun towers are being aimed by conditioned raptor cortex while a gene-spliced sauropod methane farm powers the time engine.”

## 5. Core game loop

Primary loop:

```text
Explore -> gather -> automate -> defend -> expand -> provoke stronger threats -> upgrade automation -> research return technology
```

Biopunk loop:

```text
Capture dinosaurs -> farm them -> gene-splice them -> harvest specialized outputs -> support living infrastructure -> attract worse predators -> build better defenses
```

Wetware loop:

```text
Acquire neural tissue -> grow wetware cores -> feed and condition them -> attach them to factory/defense systems -> gain adaptive automation -> manage instability
```

Defense loop:

```text
Generate disruption -> attract threats -> survive attack -> collect samples/resources -> unlock counters -> expand again
```

## 6. World and threat model

### 6.1 Disruption instead of simple pollution

The world should not use a single generic “pollution” value. Use a broader disruption system with multiple channels.

Possible disruption channels:

- noise
- heat
- light
- smoke/emissions
- ground vibration
- blood/meat scent
- prey density
- corpse scent
- chemical runoff
- electrical signatures
- temporal anomalies
- habitat destruction

Different creatures react to different channels.

Examples:

- Raptors are attracted to movement, prey density, light, and weak perimeters.
- Pterosaurs are attracted to tall towers, drones, heat, and exposed power infrastructure.
- Large theropods are attracted to blood scent, livestock density, and repeated combat.
- Sauropods are not necessarily hostile, but may destroy infrastructure during migration.
- Armored herbivores may attack if territorial zones are blocked.
- Late-game anomaly-touched creatures are attracted to temporal research infrastructure.

### 6.2 Dinosaur roles

| Creature type | Gameplay role |
|---|---|
| Small scavenger | Belt/storage raider; corpse cleanup; early nuisance |
| Raptor pack | Fast flanker; hunts players; tests turret targeting |
| Armored herbivore | Tanky wall-breaker; anti-light-turret threat |
| Pterosaur | Air threat; targets towers, drones, power lines |
| Sauropod | Neutral environmental hazard; migration disaster |
| Medium carnivore | Mid-game raid threat; attacks farms |
| Large theropod | Boss/siege threat; attracted by major disruption |
| Burrower or prehistoric reptile | Underground/infrastructure threat |
| Nest matriarch | Local territory boss |
| Chronally agitated creature | Late-game temporal escalation threat |

### 6.3 Attacks should be ecological, not arbitrary

Attacks should emerge from:

- nearby nests
- migration patterns
- predator hunger
- prey density in farms
- repeated hunting/killing
- waste and corpse handling
- industrial disruption
- temporal research stages

The player should be able to inspect or infer why an attack is happening.

## 7. Dino farming and bioindustry

### 7.1 Basic ranching

Early farms should begin with relatively normal animal-management mechanics.

Inputs:

- captured dinosaurs
- feed
- water
- fencing
- space
- player protection

Outputs:

- eggs
- manure
- hide
- bone
- fat
- blood samples
- tissue samples
- horn/plate/keratin
- methane/biofuel precursors

Risks:

- disease
- overcrowding
- predator attraction
- stampedes
- fence breakage
- corpse scent
- egg thieves
- feed shortages

### 7.2 Gene-spliced production organisms

As research advances, dinosaurs become living chemical/material factories.

Examples:

| Engineered organism | Output |
|---|---|
| Hadrosaur bio-vat line | Enzyme slurry, proteins, growth medium |
| Ankylosaur mineralization line | Ceramic armor plates, dense bone composite |
| Sauropod gut-reactor line | Methane, fertilizer, industrial acids |
| Raptor adrenal line | Combat stimulants, reflex compounds, predator pheromones |
| Pterosaur membrane line | Lightweight polymers, drone materials |
| Triceratops horn-culture line | High-density keratin composites |
| Apex predator organ line | Neural accelerants, sensory organs, advanced lures |

### 7.3 Living infrastructure

Late-game factory components can be biological.

Potential components:

- bio-reactors
- organ printers
- splice pens
- tissue culture vats
- living walls
- acid-sprayer glands
- spore mines
- thorn barriers
- neural vats
- nerve trunk data conduits
- bone-gel logic lattices
- fungal repair mats
- pheromone towers
- scent masking organs

Biotech should be powerful but messy.

A normal machine may only require power and input resources. A living production system may require:

- nutrient slurry
- water
- oxygenation
- waste filtering
- temperature control
- sedation
- immune stabilizers
- disease management
- backup containment
- predator scent suppression

Design rule:

> **Biotech is efficient, renewable, adaptive, and weird, but unstable.**

## 8. Wetware automation

### 8.1 Reason for wetware

The ship AI cannot cheaply scale advanced computing hardware in the prehistoric world. Modern semiconductor fabrication is not the intended progression path.

Instead, the AI uses dinosaur neural tissue as task-specific biological coprocessors.

In-world explanation:

> “Reconstructing high-density semiconductor manufacturing is infeasible. Local fauna, however, possess highly specialized neural architectures. With sufficient conditioning, they can serve as task-specific biological coprocessors.”

### 8.2 Wetware module types

| Wetware module | Automation role | Bias/risk |
|---|---|---|
| Raptor targeting core | Turret tracking, target leading, flanker prediction | Over-prioritizes fast movement; may waste ammo |
| Pack-hunter cluster | Multi-turret coordination, anti-swarm targeting | Can become overstimulated in large attacks |
| Pterosaur flight cortex | Drone navigation, aerial scouting, wind correction | Panic during storms; nesting behavior |
| Herd logistics core | Conveyor/drone routing, congestion avoidance | Prioritizes group safety over throughput |
| Ankylosaur defense core | Wall/gate management, damage response | May over-lockdown and trap players outside |
| Sauropod neural array | Slow base-wide optimization, resource balancing | Huge nutrient cost; slow response |
| Predator olfactory tissue | Intrusion detection, scent-based threat warning | Attracts or resonates with predators if unstable |
| Tyrant threat core | Apex-target prediction, weak-point identification | High instability; strong predator-scent signature |

### 8.3 Wetware mechanics

Wetware modules should have:

- processing capacity
- supported inputs
- supported outputs
- instinct bias
- conditioning/training level
- nutrient consumption
- heat/waste output
- stress level
- stability level
- possible failure modes

Failure modes:

- delayed responses
- false positives
- obsession loops
- panic propagation
- aggression bleed
- mis-prioritization
- hallucinated threats
- shutdown or tissue death

### 8.4 Wetware as progression

Basic turret:

> Shoots nearest target.

Turret with raptor targeting core:

> Leads fast targets and prioritizes flankers.

Turret network with pack-hunter cluster:

> Coordinates fire and avoids wasting ammo on overkill.

Turret network with tyrant threat core:

> Identifies apex threats and weak points, enabling late-game precision weapons.

Wetware should provide adaptation, not raw damage only.

## 9. Combat and defense

### 9.1 Early game combat

The player must personally fight early threats.

Possible early gear:

- flare gun
- knife/tool melee
- improvised spear
- coil pistol
- shotgun
- crude mines
- manual sentry turret
- electric fence posts
- handheld scanner

Early defense:

- crude barricades
- manually loaded turrets
- lights
- gates
- noise decoys
- emergency ship perimeter

### 9.2 Mid-game defense

Mid-game should shift toward automated defense and logistics.

Systems:

- automated ammo production
- turret resupply belts/drones
- repair drones
- concrete/metal walls
- radar/sensor towers
- kill corridors
- farm defense perimeters
- lure/bait stations
- floodlight towers
- sonic deterrents
- tranquilizer turrets

### 9.3 Late-game defense

Late game should feel like operating a war machine.

Systems:

- railgun towers
- drone swarms
- shield domes or localized barrier systems
- bioelectric fences
- living walls
- fungal repair networks
- pheromone redirection grids
- wetware-coordinated turret networks
- automated lockdown zones
- base-wide threat control center

### 9.4 Final event

The endgame return system should require a long charging/stabilization phase.

During this phase:

- temporal disruption spikes
- all disruption channels intensify
- nests mobilize
- migration events may collide with the base
- apex predators attack
- wetware systems are stressed
- the player’s entire factory-defense network is tested

This should be the ultimate validation of the player’s base design.

## 10. Technical direction

### 10.1 Primary architecture decision

Use:

> **Unreal Engine client + Rust authoritative world server**

The Rust server owns the truth. Unreal is the visual and interactive embodiment of that truth.

Core principle:

```text
Rust = truth
Unreal = embodiment
Server = world brain
Client = senses and hands
```

### 10.2 Why server-first

Hosted multiplayer and persistent co-op worlds are part of the core fantasy. Therefore, do not build single-player first and retrofit networking later.

The server should own:

- world state
- placed buildings
- player inventories
- production state
- belts, pipes, power networks
- farms and animal populations
- wetware systems
- dino ecology
- combat outcomes
- research
- persistence
- permissions

The client should own:

- rendering
- animation
- first-person feel
- visual effects
- audio
- UI
- build previews
- local interpolation
- temporary prediction

### 10.3 Unreal vs Unity

Use Unreal for the client because the game wants:

- high-quality first-person presentation
- large dinosaurs
- strong animation
- dense outdoor environments
- combat feel
- dramatic lighting and weather
- large base attacks

The Rust server reduces dependence on the engine’s built-in multiplayer stack.

### 10.4 Development platform

Best primary development environment:

- Windows PC
- Unreal Editor on Windows
- Rust server developed in WSL2, Docker, or native Rust toolchain
- Linux-compatible server builds for hosted deployment

Mac can be used for:

- Rust server development
- tools
- light client work
- macOS testing

But Windows should be treated as the primary Unreal development platform.

## 11. Server architecture

### 11.1 Server responsibilities

The Rust server is authoritative over all shared reality.

It should handle:

- deterministic world tick
- command validation
- simulation scheduling
- entity/component state
- chunk ownership/loading
- networking
- persistence
- save/load
- player sessions
- world permissions
- combat resolution
- attack generation
- offline-world behavior

### 11.2 Multi-rate simulation

Do not run every system at the same frequency.

Suggested rates:

| System | Suggested update frequency |
|---|---:|
| Player movement/combat authority | 20-60 Hz |
| Active dinosaurs/turrets | 10-30 Hz |
| Factory machines | 1-10 Hz |
| Belts/pipes | server-summarized; client visualizes continuously |
| Ecology/disruption | 0.2-1 Hz |
| Farm growth/research | event/time based |
| Persistence snapshots | periodic + important events |

### 11.3 Simulation tiers

#### Tier 1: Strategic sim

Cheap, always-on regional simulation.

Handles:

- nests
- herds
- migrations
- predator hunger
- ecological pressure
- disruption spread
- farm population growth
- remote production summaries
- attack buildup

#### Tier 2: Factory sim

Deterministic, chunked, benchmarkable.

Handles:

- machines
- recipes
- belts
- pipes
- power
- storage
- turrets
- farms
- wetware
- resource flow

#### Tier 3: Active encounter sim

Higher-fidelity simulation around players and active attacks.

Handles:

- nearby dinosaurs
- player combat
- turret firing
- projectile/hit validation
- gates/walls taking damage
- creature targeting
- base attack events

### 11.4 Determinism

The sim core should be deterministic wherever practical.

Requirements:

- fixed-tick simulation
- seedable RNG
- stable entity IDs
- deterministic tests
- no dependence on wall-clock time inside core sim logic
- clear separation between simulation time and real time

## 12. Client architecture

### 12.1 Unreal client responsibilities

The Unreal client should:

- connect to the Rust server
- send player commands
- receive snapshots/events
- interpolate entity positions
- display factory state
- display dino state
- display combat results
- display UI and build previews
- perform local prediction for player feel
- gracefully accept server corrections

### 12.2 Avoid making Unreal Actors authoritative

Do not make every belt item, resource packet, farm animal, neural signal, or machine inventory an Unreal Actor.

Unreal Actors are presentation objects. The server state is authoritative.

Bad:

```text
Every iron plate on every belt is a replicated actor.
```

Good:

```text
Server stores compressed belt lane state.
Client reconstructs convincing moving belt visuals locally.
```

### 12.3 Visual promotion model

Distant entities can be abstract. Nearby entities become visualized.

Example:

```text
Distant herd:
  species: hadrosaur
  count: 38
  center position
  migration state
  panic level

Nearby herd:
  spawned Unreal dinosaur actors
  animation
  avoidance
  hit reactions
  local sound
```

## 13. Networking model

### 13.1 General model

The client sends commands. The server validates commands, advances the world, and sends snapshots/events.

Client to server:

```text
PlayerInput
BuildRequest
DeconstructRequest
InteractRequest
InventoryMoveRequest
FireWeaponCommand
VehicleInput
BlueprintPlacementRequest
ChatCommand
AdminCommand
```

Server to client:

```text
WorldSnapshot
EntityDelta
FactoryChunkState
CombatEvent
DamageEvent
DinoSpawnEvent
ResearchEvent
InventoryState
BuildResult
Correction
AlertEvent
```

### 13.2 Protocol approach

For the earliest prototype, prefer developer speed and debuggability.

Recommended staged approach:

1. **Milestone 0:** local TCP or WebSocket dev protocol with simple binary or JSON messages.
2. **Milestone 1-2:** versioned protocol types, still simple and easy to inspect.
3. **Milestone 3+:** evaluate QUIC, UDP, Steam networking, or another production transport.

Do not optimize the network transport before proving the server-authoritative gameplay loop.

### 13.3 Snapshot philosophy

Do not send the entire world every frame. Send only what each client needs.

Use:

- interest management by region/chunk
- entity deltas
- event streams
- occasional authoritative full snapshots
- compressed factory summaries
- local visual reconstruction

## 14. Persistence and hosted worlds

### 14.1 Hosted world model

A hosted world should map naturally to one server process/container.

Basic model:

```text
One world = one Rust server process/container
Players connect with Unreal clients
Server periodically saves snapshots
Server can hibernate when empty
Server can resume from save
```

### 14.2 Offline behavior

Default behavior should avoid punishing players for logging off.

Recommended default:

- production can continue in abstract
- farms can grow
- research can progress
- ecological pressure can accumulate
- maintenance debt can accumulate
- destructive attacks should wait until players are online

When players return, the world can surface consequences:

- predator pressure is high
- containment needs maintenance
- a major attack is forming
- feedstock is low
- a farm is overcrowded
- wetware stress is elevated

Avoid default worlds where players log in to find everything destroyed while they were away.

### 14.3 Save strategy

Use a combination of:

- periodic snapshots
- important-event snapshots
- append-only event logs for debugging/replay if feasible
- versioned save format
- migration path for old saves

## 17. Belt and logistics design principle

Belts should be compressed data, not per-item physics objects.

Possible server representation:

```rust
pub struct BeltSegment {
    pub id: EntityId,
    pub from: PortRef,
    pub to: PortRef,
    pub lanes: [BeltLane; 2],
    pub speed_units_per_tick: u16,
}

pub struct BeltLane {
    pub runs: Vec<ItemRun>,
}

pub struct ItemRun {
    pub item: ItemKind,
    pub count: u16,
    pub start_offset: u16,
    pub spacing: u16,
}
```

The Unreal client can render individual moving item meshes locally from compressed belt state.

This is essential for scale.

## 18. Milestone plan

### Milestone 0: Server-first foundation

Goal: prove the architecture.

Deliverables:

- Rust workspace created
- deterministic sim loop
- seedable world
- command queue
- snapshot generation
- basic server process
- local client/test harness
- save/load skeleton
- basic logging
- basic tests

Acceptance criteria:

- `cargo test` passes
- server starts locally
- a test client can connect
- server can create a seeded world
- server can tick the world
- server can send a snapshot
- server can save and reload world state
- deterministic simulation test proves same seed + same commands = same result

### Milestone 1: Minimal factory loop

Goal: prove resource automation.

Deliverables:

- resource node
- miner
- belt
- smelter or assembler
- storage container
- simple power generator
- machine recipes
- chunked factory tick
- compressed belt state

Acceptance criteria:

- ore moves from miner to machine to storage
- machines consume power and inputs
- output is deterministic
- far-away factory state can be represented without visual objects

### Milestone 2: Minimal defense loop

Goal: prove factory-fed combat.

Deliverables:

- wall
- turret
- ammo recipe
- turret ammo consumption
- simple raptor enemy
- raptor attack target selection
- damage resolution
- attack warning event

Acceptance criteria:

- factory can produce ammo
- turret can consume ammo and fire
- raptors can damage walls/buildings
- turret can kill raptors
- combat outcome is server-authoritative

### Milestone 3: Disruption and attack generation

Goal: prove ecological escalation.

Deliverables:

- disruption emitters
- disruption map/channel values
- nest or predator source
- attack buildup over time
- basic threat selection based on disruption

Acceptance criteria:

- larger/noisier base increases attack likelihood
- different disruption channels can influence threat type
- attack reason can be inspected/debugged

### Milestone 4: Dino farming

Goal: prove dinosaurs as resources.

Deliverables:

- farm pen building
- captured herbivore entity/population
- feed and water requirements
- manure/egg/biomass production
- prey-density disruption
- predator attraction from farms

Acceptance criteria:

- farm produces bio-resources over time
- farm requires inputs
- farm increases predator pressure
- predators can attack farm structures or animals

### Milestone 5: Biopunk production

Goal: prove engineered creatures as industrial systems.

Deliverables:

- splice lab
- simple engineered output line
- nutrient slurry input
- biological waste output
- instability/stress value
- disease or failure event placeholder

Acceptance criteria:

- engineered organism/building consumes biological inputs
- produces a specialized material/chemical
- has stability constraints
- creates a distinct disruption profile

### Milestone 6: Wetware automation

Goal: prove neural tissue as a functional automation upgrade.

Deliverables:

- wetware vat
- raptor targeting core
- nutrient/sedative inputs
- turret network integration
- targeting modifier
- stress/failure behavior placeholder

Acceptance criteria:

- turret without wetware uses simple targeting
- turret with raptor core leads/prioritizes fast targets better
- wetware consumes upkeep resources
- wetware can become stressed if under-supplied

### Milestone 7: Unreal thin client vertical slice

Goal: see and play the server-authoritative loop.

Deliverables:

- Unreal project
- connection to Rust server
- basic player movement
- build preview
- place miner/belt/turret through server command
- visualize server snapshots
- spawn simple raptor actor from server state
- display combat events

Acceptance criteria:

- Unreal client can connect to local Rust server
- player can place a small factory-defense setup
- server validates build commands
- client visualizes production and attack
- raptor death/wall damage/turret fire are server-authoritative

### Milestone 8: Hosted multiplayer prototype

Goal: prove hosted co-op.

Deliverables:

- two clients connect to one server
- shared world state
- player identity/session handling
- world save persists across restart
- basic permissions
- simple deployment script/container

Acceptance criteria:

- two players see same world
- both can build/interact
- server remains authoritative
- restart preserves world
- hosted-like local deployment works

- `cargo test` at workspace root
- clear module boundaries
- no Unreal dependency in Rust sim crates
- seedable deterministic RNG abstraction
- basic README explaining each crate

### Task 2: Implement deterministic simulation skeleton

In `dino_sim`, implement:

- `WorldState`
- `WorldConfig`
- `SimulationTime`
- `TickContext`
- `PlayerCommand`
- deterministic command application
- fixed tick function: `tick_world(world: &mut WorldState, commands: &[PlayerCommand])`

Add tests:

- same seed + same commands produces same hash
- different commands produce different state
- tick count advances predictably

### Task 3: Implement initial protocol types

In `dino_protocol`, implement:

- client-to-server command enum
- server-to-client event enum
- world snapshot struct
- entity ID type
- protocol version field

For Milestone 0, use a simple serialization approach that is easy to replace later.

### Task 4: Implement local server process

In `dino_server`, implement:

- starts a local development server
- creates or loads a world
- accepts at least one local test client
- receives commands
- advances simulation
- sends periodic snapshots
- logs tick rate and connected clients

Do not over-optimize. The purpose is to prove the loop.

### Task 5: Implement persistence skeleton

In `dino_persistence`, implement:

- save world snapshot to disk
- load world snapshot from disk
- include save version
- include world seed
- include world tick

Add tests for save/load round trip.

### Task 6: Implement a CLI test client

In `dino_tools`, implement a basic CLI client that can:

- connect to local server
- print snapshots/events
- send a small scripted sequence of commands
- run a deterministic smoke test

This client is temporary but important. It lets the server be tested before Unreal integration.

## 20. Engineering guardrails

### 20.1 Do this

- Keep the Rust sim pure and testable.
- Write deterministic tests from the beginning.
- Keep protocol messages versioned.
- Separate simulation, networking, persistence, and presentation.
- Use stable IDs and explicit state transitions.
- Design for server authority.
- Keep early milestones small and vertical.
- Build debug tools early.

### 20.2 Avoid this

- Do not make Unreal Actors the source of truth.
- Do not simulate every belt item as a networked object.
- Do not build a huge open world before proving the factory-defense loop.
- Do not implement complex dinosaur animation before server combat works.
- Do not build production-grade networking before gameplay is proven.
- Do not add dozens of resources before the first complete resource loop works.
- Do not let offline simulation destroy a player’s base by default.
- Do not create a “modern chip fab” tech tree; use wetware and biopunk instead.

## 24. Open design questions

These should be explored after the first server/factory/combat loop exists.

3. How cruel/dark should the biopunk systems feel?
4. Should wetware be visually grotesque, clinical, or both?
5. How much should the ship AI push ethically questionable choices?

9. How many players should one hosted world support initially?

## 25. Suggested tone and art direction

The tone should mix:

- awe
- danger
- dark humor
- survival tension
- industrial satisfaction
- biopunk unease

Visual progression:

### Early base

Metal salvage, crude walls, smoke, lights, emergency ship tech.

### Mid base

Conveyors, farms, electric fencing, hatcheries, slurry pipes, tissue labs, turret lines.

### Late base

Steel, concrete, bone composites, glowing vats, pulsing nerve conduits, living walls, drone bays, wetware towers, railguns, time-engine pylons.

## 26. Ship AI personality

The AI should be useful, calm, dry, and slightly unsettling.

Example lines:

> “The approaching predator is approximately nine tons. Your current perimeter is rated for goats.”

> “Reconstructing high-density semiconductor manufacturing is infeasible. Fortunately, the local ecosystem has already manufactured several tons of task-specialized neural tissue.”

> “Raptor cortex integration successful. Turret accuracy increased by 31%. Turret empathy remains zero.”

> “Predator incursion probability has increased by 47%. Congratulations: your ranching program is ecologically visible.”

> “The logistics brain has developed a preference for keeping all haulers in groups of seven. This is inefficient, but adorable.”

## 27. Final design summary

The game should not be only about building a factory in dinosaur times. It should be about using a damaged future AI, prehistoric ecology, industrial logistics, and increasingly questionable biotech to build a fortress capable of surviving the consequences of its own growth.

The central tension:

> **Can your automation keep up with the consequences of your automation?**

The central identity:

> **A hosted multiplayer, first-person factory-defense game where the factory becomes part machine, part engineered ecosystem, and part nervous system.**
